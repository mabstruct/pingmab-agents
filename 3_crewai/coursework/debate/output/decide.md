The opposition is more convincing. The proposition persuasively argues that routine obedience is essential to legal stability and that personal disagreement cannot automatically excuse lawbreaking. However, it does not adequately address laws that directly impose grave oppression.

The opposition provides decisive historical counterexamples—slavery, segregation, and persecution—showing that unconditional obedience can make citizens complicit in serious injustice. It also avoids treating every personal grievance as sufficient by setting limits for justified civil disobedience: the injustice must be serious, resistance proportionate and preferably nonviolent, directed toward reform, and undertaken with acceptance of legal consequences. This directly answers the proposition’s concern that conscience would otherwise become a blanket excuse for extremism or selfish evasion.

Therefore, while citizens have a strong general duty to obey the law, the opposition better establishes that this duty cannot be absolute when laws command grave injustice.
