Citizens have a duty to obey even laws they believe unjust because the rule of law cannot survive if obedience depends on personal approval. Every law is considered unfair by someone. If individual conscience alone were enough to cancel legal obligations, tax evaders, political extremists, and violent groups could all excuse themselves by claiming moral certainty. Law would cease to be a common standard and become merely optional advice.

This duty is especially important in a democracy. Citizens benefit from public order, courts, infrastructure, and rights protected by the legal system; in return, they must accept lawful decisions even when they lose the argument. Democratic legitimacy means resolving disagreements through shared institutions—not allowing each person to appoint themselves judge, jury, and legislator.

Obedience does not require silence or moral agreement. Citizens should criticize unjust laws, campaign against them, challenge them in court, vote out their authors, and seek repeal. But reform must occur through lawful processes. Otherwise, the principle used by the righteous dissenter today becomes the excuse used by the dangerous fanatic tomorrow.

A stable and free society therefore depends on a clear priority: conscience may guide our efforts to change the law, but it cannot by itself grant permission to disregard it. Obedience must come first, because equal law is only possible when no individual places personal judgment above the rules binding everyone.
