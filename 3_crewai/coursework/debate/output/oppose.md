Citizens have a general duty to respect the law—but not an absolute duty to obey injustice. Law derives its moral authority from protecting human dignity and equal rights. When it instead commands oppression, obedience can make citizens participants in wrongdoing.

History proves the danger of putting legality above conscience. Slavery, racial segregation, and persecution were all enforced through valid laws. Those who resisted such laws were not enemies of justice; they helped restore it. If obedience must always come first, then helping an escaped slave, defying segregation, or sheltering a persecuted minority becomes morally wrong simply because the state forbids it. That conclusion is indefensible.

This does not mean every private grievance excuses lawbreaking. Justified civil disobedience should confront a serious injustice, remain proportionate and preferably nonviolent, pursue reform openly, and accept legal consequences. These standards distinguish principled resistance from selfish evasion or violent extremism.

Democracy is more than majority rule and official procedure; it also rests on rights that majorities must not violate. Courts and elections can fail, exclude victims, or act too slowly while real harm continues. The rule of law deserves loyalty only when it remains connected to justice—it must not become rule *by* law.

Citizens should normally obey, but conscience cannot be surrendered to the state. When law commands grave injustice, the higher civic duty is not blind compliance but principled resistance.
